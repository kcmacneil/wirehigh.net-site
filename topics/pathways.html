Pathways are the part of the plant nobody re-does. Trays, ladder rack and conduits get installed at build time and then filled by every subsequent project, so fill ratio, separation and access are worth arguing about early.

Route options

  • Overhead ladder rack. Strong, open and easy to inspect; the default for copper and fiber trunks above the cabinets, usually on two or three tiers to separate services.
  • Wire basket tray. Lighter and cheaper, good for branch runs, with edge protection where cable leaves the basket.
  • Fiber raceway. Enclosed, radius-controlled ducting for patch cords between patch fields, protecting cords from crushing and over-bending.
  • Underfloor. Still used where a raised floor exists, but it competes with cooling airflow and hides abandoned cable — keep it for power where possible.

Sizing and separation

  • Fill ratio. Design to roughly 25 to 40 percent fill at handover so later projects have somewhere to go; a full tray forces cable to be laid on top of cable, crushing what is below.
  • Layering. Separate fiber, copper and power onto different tiers or trays. Fiber on top, never under a copper bundle.
  • Power separation. Maintain the separation your standard requires between unshielded copper and power runs, and cross at right angles when a crossing is unavoidable.
  • Bend and drop management. Use radius drops and waterfalls where cable leaves containment into a cabinet; a sharp tray edge is a slow-motion failure.

Housekeeping

  • Support cable at the specified interval — no cable resting on ceiling grid, pipework or sprinkler heads.
  • Bond metallic pathways per code; containment is part of the grounding system.
  • Remove abandoned cable. It is a fire-load and pathway-capacity problem, and codes increasingly require its removal.
