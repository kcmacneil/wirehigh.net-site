A transmission line is a set of bare conductors held in air at a safe distance from everything else. Almost every design decision follows from two numbers: the voltage it must insulate against and the span it must cross.

Design drivers

  • Voltage class. Insulator length, phase spacing and clearance to ground scale with operating voltage. Raising a line from 115 kV to 230 kV changes the structure, not just the hardware.
  • Conductor selection. ACSR, ACSS and all-aluminium conductors trade strength, sag behaviour and thermal rating. Ampacity is set by the maximum allowed operating temperature, not by copper-style rules of thumb.
  • Sag and tension. Conductors are strung to a tension that keeps sag inside the clearance envelope at maximum temperature and under ice and wind loading.
  • Structure loading. Lattice towers, tubular steel poles and wood structures are checked for vertical, transverse and longitudinal loads, including broken-conductor cases.

Construction sequence

  • Route and survey. LiDAR or ground survey fixes the profile, crossings and constraints; the plan-and-profile drawing is the contract between design and the crew.
  • Foundations and framing. Drilled piers or direct-embed poles are set, then structures are framed and dressed with insulators and hardware on the ground where possible.
  • Stringing. Sock lines pull the conductor through travellers under controlled tension so it never touches the ground or an energised crossing.
  • Sagging and clipping. Spans are sagged to the stringing chart for the day's temperature, then clipped in, and dampers and spacers are installed.

What goes wrong

  • Clearance encroachment from conductor creep, vegetation growth or new construction under the line.
  • Hardware wear at suspension points — worn armour rod, cracked insulators, corroded fittings.
  • Aeolian vibration and galloping in long spans without adequate damping.
